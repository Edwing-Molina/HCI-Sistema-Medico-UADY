# Psychology in software development

We, as software developers, have enormous power that we rarely question: every technical, architectural, or UI decision can end up directly influencing people’s mental health, self‑esteem, and the way they relate to themselves and others.  

For many years, the focus of development has been on functionality, scalability, and performance, almost as if the user were an abstract actor. However, today we know that the way we present a notification, a “likes” counter, an error message, or even a delete‑account button can generate anxiety, shame, frustration, or a sense of loss of control.  

An example like the “like” on Facebook shows how a single design element, conceived to increase engagement, can become an axis of addiction, social comparison, and low self‑esteem. As developers, if we do not ask ourselves *what emotion we want to provoke* or *what habit we are reinforcing* when we design a notification, an infinite feed, or a reward system based on usage, we are essentially working blindfolded on people’s psyche.  

There are already serious discussions about “ethical design” and “addictive design,” highlighting how many platforms exploit psychological principles to retain users, even at the expense of their well‑being. As developers, entering this debate means looking at each project not only from the backlog or the KPIs, but also from the question: *Do we really want this app to become essential in the user’s life, or merely useful?*  

In serious projects related to mental health, education, or digital therapies, it is common for psychologists, psychiatrists, or emotional‑UX experts to work alongside the technical team. That approach deserves to be extended to any app that has social impact: social networks, finance apps, hybrid‑work tools, commerce platforms, and so on. Including psychological and UX perspectives in our teams is not just a “nice extra”; it is a way to prevent invisible harms.  

We are also users of technology and social media, and we often reproduce in our own systems the same kind of design that makes us sick: aggressive notifications, constant metrics, short‑term rewards without reflection. Here, a double responsibility arises: to take care of our own mental health as developers and, at the same time, to commit ourselves to more conscious design—one that not only “works,” but also respects people’s dignity, autonomy, and emotional fragility.  

In practice, this could mean:  
- running usability tests focused on stress and anxiety, not just click speed.  
- questioning features that increase usage time without a clear benefit to the user.  
- designing “dignified exits”: making it easy to leave campaigns, groups, or notifications without technical penalties or psychological pressure.  

<mark>At its core, the idea is simple yet profound: good code is no longer enough; we also need a good heart and a solid understanding of the human psyche, because every app we create is, in a way, a small mental environment where people live part of their lives.<mark>
