# Reflection: Tracing Accessibility in Our Project

**Core Idea: Accessibility is not an optional feature but the foundation of effective design, especially in systems meant to serve diverse users, including older adults.**

## Reflection

**Claim (Main Argument):**  
For our medical application to truly work, it’s not enough for it to look modern—it has to be accessible. Accessibility is not an “extra,” but the core of the entire design. If a UADY worker who isn’t familiar with technology cannot read their prescription or schedule an appointment, then the app—no matter how visually appealing—has failed its main purpose.

**Data (Evidence):**  
We chose **Accessibility** as our primary quality attribute, and this translated into concrete design decisions:

- We used **high-contrast colors** to ensure text is easy to read.
- We designed **larger buttons with wider touch areas**, considering users who may struggle with precision on touchscreens.
- We kept navigation simple, avoiding hidden menus or complex steps that could confuse users who don’t frequently use mobile devices.

**Warrant (Reasoning):**  
To evaluate whether our design actually works, we relied on an international standard: **WCAG 2.1 Level AA**. This serves as a benchmark that connects our intention (supporting older users) with measurable and verifiable outcomes. It ensures that our design decisions are not arbitrary but aligned with proven accessibility principles.

**Backing (Additional Support):**  
Using a standard like WCAG gives us confidence in our design. By validating elements such as **color contrast** (minimum 4.5:1 ratio) and text size, we are applying globally tested guidelines. This prevents us from designing based on personal preference and instead pushes us to design based on what is effective for real users.

**Qualifier (Limitations):**  
Although meeting these technical standards is a strong step forward, it does not guarantee that older users will fully understand the app on their first interaction. Technical compliance is the baseline, but real usability must be validated through actual user interaction.

**Rebuttal (Counterargument):**  
Some might argue that following accessibility standards is tedious or limits creativity. However, in a medical project—especially one tied to UADY—the priority is clarity and usability, not artistic experimentation. Without these metrics, design decisions would rely on guesswork, which is risky in healthcare contexts.

## Team Project Example

In our case, accessibility decisions were directly tied to our goals. Every design choice—such as increasing button size or improving contrast—was intentional and traceable to a specific need: ensuring that no user, especially older adults, is left behind.

If we wanted to strengthen our evaluation even further, we could complement technical validation with real user data, such as:

1. **Task completion time:** How long does it take a 60-year-old user to schedule an appointment compared to a 20-year-old?  
2. **Error rate:** How often do users tap the wrong button?  
3. **User satisfaction surveys:** Asking simple questions like, “Did the text feel too small or difficult to read?”  

Ultimately, traceability means being able to justify every design decision: “We made this button this size because our goal is accessibility, and we validated it using an international standard.” This is what brings structure and meaning to our work as developers.
